🦴 Human Evolution

A complete second-grade lesson plan with hands-on projects, assessments, and resources

📚 Grade 2 (Ages 7–8) 🔬 Science / Social Studies ⏱️ 4 Sessions · ~45 min each 🧪 3 Hands-On Projects

📋 Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

  • Explain in simple terms what "evolution" means and why living things change over time.
  • Name at least three ways early humans were different from, and similar to, modern humans.
  • Sequence key milestones in human evolution on a visual timeline.
  • Identify traits that make humans unique — upright walking, large brains, tool use, and language.
  • Connect the idea that humans share common ancestors with other primates.
📝 A Note for Parents: At this grade level the goal is wonder, not memorization. Use age-appropriate language — "our ancient relatives," "long, long ago," "over millions of years." Encourage questions and celebrate curiosity above all else.

🔤 Key Vocabulary

Words to Learn

  • Evolution – how living things change slowly over a very long time
  • Ancestor – a relative who lived a long time before you
  • Primate – a group of mammals that includes monkeys, apes, and humans
  • Fossil – the hardened remains or imprint of a plant or animal preserved in rock
  • Bipedal – walking upright on two legs
  • Tool – an object used to do a job (sticks, rocks, hammers)
  • Trait – a feature or characteristic of a living thing

✅ Pre-lesson Check-in Questions

  • What do you think you look like compared to animals?
  • Have you ever seen a fossil? What was it?
  • Where do you think the very first people came from?
  • What makes humans different from other animals?
  • How do you think animals change over time?

📅 Session Overview

1

What Is Evolution?

  • Introduce the word "evolution" with a picture book
  • Compare baby photos to present-day photos → bodies change!
  • Read aloud: Our Family Tree by Lisa Peters
  • Discussion: "How do you think dogs became so many different sizes?"
  • Draw a "then vs. now" picture of an animal
2

Our Primate Cousins

  • Show photos of chimps, gorillas, and humans side-by-side
  • Compare hands, feet, and faces — spot similarities and differences
  • Thumb challenge activity (tape one thumb down!)
  • Introduce the idea of a "family tree" for all living things
  • Mini quiz: "Human or ape? You decide!" photo sort
3

Early Humans & Tools

  • View age-appropriate images of Homo habilis, Homo erectus, modern humans
  • Discuss walking upright — how does it help us?
  • Simple tools then vs. now: stone flake → metal knife → robot arm
  • Watch a 5-min video: "Early Humans for Kids" (YouTube)
  • Class discussion: "What tools do you use every day?"
4

Project Day & Wrap-up

  • Complete the Human Evolution Timeline Booklet project
  • Share and present timelines to a family member
  • Play "Evolution Bingo" to review vocabulary
  • Answer the pre-lesson questions again — what changed?
  • Celebration: "I'm a young scientist!" certificate

🧪 Hands-On Projects

These projects make abstract concepts tangible. Each takes 20–40 minutes and uses inexpensive materials.

⭐ Project 1: Human Evolution Timeline Booklet (Featured / Main Project)

✂️ Arts & Crafts 🕐 35–45 min 📚 Sequencing 🎨 Creative

Students create a fold-out accordion booklet that tells the story of human evolution through illustrations and simple captions — from ancient ape-like ancestors to modern humans. Each page represents one key stage.

🛒 Materials Needed
  • 1 sheet of white cardstock or 4 sheets of white paper (cut to 4"×6" cards)
  • Crayons, colored pencils, or markers
  • Tape or a stapler
  • Scissors
  • Printed reference images of early humans (freely available via Smithsonian website)
  • A pencil for writing captions

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Fold a long piece of paper accordion-style into 5 equal sections (or tape 5 cards end-to-end). This is your "timeline."
  2. Write one stage name on each panel: Panel 1 – Ancient Ancestor (ape-like, knuckle-walking), Panel 2 – Australopithecus ("Lucy" — small, starting to walk upright), Panel 3 – Homo habilis (first stone tool user), Panel 4 – Homo erectus (used fire, traveled far), Panel 5 – Homo sapiens (that's us! art, language, science).
  3. Draw a simple figure for each stage. Use the reference images as a guide. It's okay if the figures are stick-people — focus on one or two key traits per stage (e.g., knuckle-walking vs. walking upright, no tools vs. stone tools).
  4. Write one sentence (or have the parent scribe) under each figure explaining what makes that stage special.
  5. On the back of the booklet write: "Millions of years of change — all leading to ME!" and decorate the cover.
  6. Share your booklet and "walk" someone through human history!
💡 Extension: Add arrows between panels and label the most important change between each stage (e.g., "Started walking upright →" or "Made first tools →").

🖐️ Project 2: The Opposable Thumb Challenge

🔬 Experiment 🕐 20–25 min 🤔 Critical Thinking

One of the most important human traits is the opposable thumb — it lets us grip and manipulate tools in ways most animals cannot. In this experiment, students discover this by trying everyday tasks without using their thumbs.

🛒 Materials Needed
  • Medical tape or painter's tape (gentle on skin)
  • A pencil and piece of paper
  • Building blocks or LEGO pieces
  • A zipper bag or jar with a lid
  • A fork and a piece of soft food (e.g., banana slices)
  • Observation worksheet (see below)

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Before starting, predict: "Which task will be hardest without your thumb?"
  2. Tape both thumbs gently to the side of each hand so they cannot be used.
  3. Try each task below and rate difficulty 1–3 (1 = easy, 3 = very hard): Write your name · Stack 5 blocks · Open a zipper bag · Pick up food with a fork · Turn a page in a book
  4. Remove the tape. Try the same tasks again with thumbs free.
  5. Fill in the observation table: "With thumb" vs. "Without thumb" difficulty rating.
  6. Discuss: "Why do you think having a good grip made early humans better at surviving?"
💡 Discussion Prompt: Ask your child — "If you were an early human and needed to make a sharp stone tool to cut food, how important would your thumb be? What might happen if you didn't have one?"

🦶 Project 3: Fossil Footprint Cast

🧱 Craft / STEM 🕐 40 min + 24 hr dry time 🔎 Observation

Fossils are how scientists learn about early humans. In this project, students make their own footprint "fossil" using air-dry clay or salt dough and compare a flat-footed, bipedal human footprint with a model of a knuckle-walking footprint (made by a fist).

🛒 Materials Needed
  • Air-dry clay (one small pack) OR salt-dough recipe (1 cup flour + ½ cup salt + ½ cup water)
  • A rolling pin or smooth cup to flatten clay
  • A plastic tray or sheet of parchment paper
  • Washable paint (optional, for display)
  • A permanent marker for labeling

Step-by-Step Instructions

  1. Roll out two palm-sized slabs of clay about 1 cm thick.
  2. On the first slab, press your bare foot firmly to create a human bipedal footprint. Label it: "Homo sapiens – walks upright".
  3. On the second slab, press your closed fist (knuckles down) firmly to create a knuckle-walking imprint. Label it: "Ancient ancestor – knuckle-walks".
  4. Set both slabs aside to air-dry for 24 hours (or bake salt dough at 200°F / 95°C for 2 hours).
  5. Once dry, paint them if desired and compare them side-by-side.
  6. Write or dictate two observations: "The human footprint shows _____ because _____."
💡 Science Connection: In 1978, scientist Mary Leakey discovered fossilized bipedal footprints in Tanzania that are about 3.6 million years old! Show your child a picture of the "Laetoli footprints" and compare them to your clay fossil.

📊 Assessment Ideas

🗣️ Oral / Discussion

  • Ask: "Can you tell me one way early humans were different from us?"
  • Ask: "What is a fossil and why is it useful?"
  • Have your child explain their timeline booklet to another person
  • Play vocabulary "flash-card" game — define the word or use it in a sentence
  • "Two facts, one question" exit ticket — write two things you learned and one question you still have

✏️ Written / Visual

  • Label a diagram of a human skeleton with 3 traits unique to humans
  • Write 2–3 sentences: "What I learned about human evolution is…"
  • Sequence picture cards — put 5 evolution stages in order
  • Draw and label "Early Human" vs. "Modern Human"
  • Complete the sentence: "Humans are special because ______."

🏆 Rubric (Simple)

  • ⭐⭐⭐ Excellent: Can name 3+ stages, uses vocabulary correctly, project complete
  • ⭐⭐ Good: Names 2 stages, uses some vocabulary, project mostly complete
  • ⭐ Developing: Names 1 stage with help, limited vocabulary use
  • Focus on effort and curiosity — celebrate all progress!

🎉 Celebration Ideas

  • Print a "Junior Paleontologist Certificate" for your child
  • Display the timeline booklet and fossil casts on a "Science Museum Wall" at home
  • Have your child give a 2-minute "museum tour" presentation to family
  • Watch a dinosaur or evolution documentary together as a reward

🏠 Home Extension Activities: Visit a local natural history museum if one is nearby — even a virtual tour of the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History is excellent. Make a "Family Tree of Life" poster together showing how humans, chimps, dogs, birds, and fish are all connected through ancient common ancestors. Start a nature journal — go outside, observe animals, and ask "How might this animal have changed over millions of years?"
